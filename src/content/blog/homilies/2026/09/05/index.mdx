## 讲道 | Homily

今天福音里发生的事情其实很小：耶稣和门徒经过麦田，门徒饿了，就掐下几穗麦子，用手搓着吃。

没有人偷窃，没有人大摆宴席，也没有人故意挑战安息日。几个饥饿的人，只是在路上吃了一点麦粒。

但法利塞人看到的不是“人饿了”，而是“有人违反了规定”。

于是，耶稣最后说了一句非常重要的话：

**“人子也是安息日的主。”**

这句话并不是说规矩不重要。耶稣没有反对安息日。恰恰相反，他是在告诉我们：**必须记得天主为什么给人安息日。**

安息日不是一个陷阱，不是为了寻找谁做错了一件小事；安息日原本就是天主给人的礼物——让人停止劳作，让人休息，让人重新记起自己不是机器，也不是工作的奴隶，而是天主所爱的儿女。

问题出现在什么时候？

当我们越来越认真地维护一条规则，却慢慢忘记这条规则最初是为了保护什么人的时候。

法利塞人非常熟悉法律，却没有首先看见门徒的饥饿。

这种事情并没有离我们很远。

今年夏天，马耳他的卫生部门多次提醒，在极端高温期间，应避免在最炎热的时间进行剧烈户外活动，并特别指出户外工作人员面临更大的健康风险。马耳他近年来也进一步制定措施，要求在高温条件下为工人提供饮水、遮阴和休息空间；在严重天气条件下，某些户外工作甚至必须暂停。

这些规定背后的逻辑，其实很容易理解：

**工作很重要，但人比工作重要。
规则很重要，但人的生命是规则所要保护的对象。**

如果有人在四十度的烈日下工作，我们不能只说：“合同规定你今天应该工作。”

我们必须先问：“这个人安全吗？”

这正是今天福音给我们的眼光。

我们也许不会像法利塞人一样讨论能不能在安息日掐麦穗，但我们每天都可能犯同样的错误。

在公司里，我们可能说：“流程就是这样。”
在家庭里，我们可能说：“规矩就是规矩。”
在教会里，我们也可能说：“一直以来都是这样做的。”

这些话本身未必错。

但基督今天要求我们再多问一句：

**这个流程、这个规定、这个传统，现在是在帮助人，还是已经变成了压在人身上的重担？**

特别值得注意的是，耶稣没有为了显示自己宽容，就把一切规则取消。

他引用了达味的故事。

换句话说，耶稣不是反对法律，而是在解释法律真正的精神。

基督徒的成熟，不是“什么都可以”，也不是“规定写什么我就机械地做什么”。

真正成熟的信仰，是能够分辨：

什么时候应该坚持原则，
什么时候应该给予宽容；
什么时候必须说“不”，
什么时候必须先看见一个人的饥饿、疲惫、痛苦和需要。

我们在判断一个人以前，可以养成一个很简单的习惯：

**先问发生了什么，再问他违反了什么。**

一个同事今天工作做得不好，也许他正在经历家庭困难。

一个孩子突然变得不听话，也许他真正需要的是有人听他说话。

一个人在教会里没有按照我们熟悉的方式行事，也许他才刚刚开始认识信仰。

如果我们只看到错误，我们很容易成为今天福音中的法利塞人。

但如果我们首先看到人，我们就开始用基督的眼睛看世界。

安息日最终提醒我们一件事：

**人不是为了制度而存在；制度应当帮助人走向天主。**

所以今天，我们可以问自己：

当我面对别人的错误时，我首先想到的是“规则”，还是“这个人”？

如果基督今天经过我们的家庭、办公室、堂区，他也许会提醒我们：

不要只看那几穗被掐下来的麦子。

先看看那个饥饿的人。

What happens in today’s Gospel is actually very small.

Jesus and his disciples are walking through a field. The disciples are hungry, so they pluck a few heads of grain, rub them in their hands, and eat.

No one is stealing. No one is holding a feast. No one is deliberately trying to provoke anyone.

They are simply hungry.

Yet when the Pharisees look at the scene, they do not first see hungry people.

They see a broken rule.

Jesus therefore ends the exchange with an extraordinary statement:

**“The Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath.”**

Jesus is not saying that rules do not matter. He is not rejecting the Sabbath.

He is reminding them why God gave the Sabbath in the first place.

The Sabbath was not designed as a trap for catching people doing something wrong. It was given as a gift: a time to stop working, to rest, and to remember that a human being is not a machine and not a slave to work, but a child of God.

The danger begins when we become so determined to defend a rule that we forget the person whom the rule was originally meant to protect.

The Pharisees knew the law very well.

But they failed to notice hunger.

That problem is not far removed from our own world.

During the intense heat in Malta this summer, the health authorities again warned people to avoid strenuous outdoor activity during the hottest hours and specifically highlighted the risks faced by people working outdoors. Malta has also introduced measures requiring protection such as drinking water, shade and rest areas for workers exposed to extreme heat, and in severe weather certain outdoor work may have to stop altogether.

The principle behind those measures is simple:

**Work matters, but the worker matters more.
Rules matter, but human life is what the rules are supposed to protect.**

If somebody is working under extreme heat, it is not enough to say, “Your contract says you are supposed to work today.”

We first have to ask: “Is this person safe?”

That is the vision Jesus gives us in today's Gospel.

We may never argue about whether someone is allowed to pluck grain on the Sabbath, but we can easily repeat the same mistake.

At work we say, “That is the procedure.”

At home we say, “Those are the rules.”

Even in the Church we can say, “This is how we have always done it.”

Sometimes those statements are perfectly correct.

But Christ asks us to add another question:

**Is this rule helping the person before me, or has it become a burden placed upon him?**

Jesus does not simply abolish the law. He points to the story of David.

In other words, Jesus teaches us how to understand the purpose of the law.

Christian maturity therefore does not mean saying that everything is permitted. Neither does it mean mechanically applying every regulation without discernment.

Christian maturity means learning to distinguish:

when a principle must be defended,
and when mercy must be shown;

when we must say no,
and when we must first notice someone's hunger, exhaustion, suffering or need.

There is one very practical habit we can take from today's Gospel:

**Before asking what rule a person has broken, ask what has happened to that person.**

Perhaps a colleague who performs badly today is struggling with a serious problem at home.

Perhaps a child who suddenly becomes difficult does not first need punishment, but someone who will listen.

Perhaps someone who behaves awkwardly in church is only beginning to discover the faith.

If we see only the mistake, we can very quickly become like the Pharisees in today's Gospel.

If we first see the person, we begin to see the world through the eyes of Christ.

The Sabbath ultimately reminds us of something fundamental:

**Human beings were not created for systems. Systems should help human beings return to God.**

So today we can ask ourselves:

When someone makes a mistake, what do I see first—the rule, or the person?

If Christ walked today through our homes, workplaces and parishes, perhaps he would tell us:

Do not look only at the few heads of grain that were plucked.

First look at the person who is hungry.

## 今日回应 | Today's Response

今天遇到一个做错事、没有达到要求、或让自己不满意的人时，在批评以前先问自己一句：

**“他现在真正需要什么？”**

Today, when someone makes a mistake, fails to meet an expectation, or disappoints us, ask one question before criticizing:

**“What does this person actually need right now?”** 🟥
