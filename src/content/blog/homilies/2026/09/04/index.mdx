## 今日讲道 · Homily

### 中文

今天的福音里，人们向耶稣提出一个看起来很合理的问题：为什么若翰的门徒禁食，法利塞人的门徒也禁食，而你的门徒却吃喝呢？

问题表面上是在谈禁食，实际上是在问：**一个真正虔诚的人，应该是什么样子？**

他们已经有了一套熟悉的标准。他们知道什么时候该禁食，知道虔诚应该有哪些外在表现，也知道如何判断别人是否符合这些标准。可是耶稣没有直接讨论“禁食多一点好，还是少一点好”。祂把问题带到了更深的地方：**新郎就在这里。**

这才是整个福音的中心。

基督信仰首先不是一套规则，而是一个人的临在——耶稣基督。禁食、祈祷、守斋、礼仪、善工当然重要，但如果我们忘记这些事情最终是为了使我们走近基督，那么最好的宗教习惯也可能变成一个空壳。

因此，耶稣接着说到新布、旧衣服，新酒、旧皮囊。

我们很容易把“新酒装在新皮囊里”理解成一句简单的口号：新的就是好的，旧的就应该被淘汰。但耶稣最后偏偏又加了一句：喝惯旧酒的人会说：“还是旧的好。”

这句话非常真实。

我们喜欢熟悉的东西。我们喜欢自己已经掌握的祈祷方式，喜欢熟悉的弥撒时间，喜欢自己认识的人，喜欢“我们一直都是这样做的”。甚至在个人生活中也是如此。我们希望天主帮助我们，但最好不要改变我们太多；我们希望耶稣进入我们的生活，却希望祂适应我们原来的生活安排。

于是我们常常做一件事：**不是让福音改变自己，而是从福音上剪下一块布，补在原来的生活上。**

星期天去弥撒，但星期一做生意的方法不变。

祈求天主宽恕自己，却继续抓住别人过去的错误不放。

在圣堂里说“愿你的旨意奉行”，回到家却坚持所有人都必须照我的意思做。

读了耶稣关于穷人、陌生人和弱小者的话，却仍然把自己不喜欢的人迅速归类、判断、排斥。

这样的信仰，很像把新布补在旧衣服上。表面上多了一点“宗教”，里面的结构却没有改变。

真正困难的，并不是接受一条新的教训，而是允许天主重新塑造那个承载福音的“皮囊”——我们的习惯、判断方式、关系、时间安排、金钱观念，甚至我们对“我是怎样一个人”的理解。

这一点在今天的马耳他教会身上也特别值得思考。

马耳他总教区正在制定和落实 **2026–2029 牧灵计划**。目前正处于各教会机构制定具体实施计划的阶段，正式实施预计从今年十月开始。值得注意的是，总教区自己特别说明，这个计划的重点并不是增加大量新的活动，而是推动一种“质的更新”：重新审视教会文化、牧灵实践、现有结构以及彼此合作的方式，使教会在今天的马耳他社会中仍能真实地活出共融、参与和使命。

这其实很像“新酒和新皮囊”的问题。

教会当然不能因为“新”就抛弃传统。圣事、信经、圣经、宗徒传下来的信仰，并不是需要丢掉的旧皮囊。相反，我们所领受的信仰正是珍贵的宝藏。

真正需要不断更新的，是**我们承载和活出这信仰的方式**。

如果社会已经改变，而我们的牧灵方式永远不肯改变；如果马耳他的堂区里已经出现越来越多来自不同国家、语言和文化背景的天主教徒，而我们仍然只按照过去单一文化的方式思考“我们的堂区”；如果平信徒拥有许多恩赐，却始终只把教会的使命理解为神父和修会人士的工作，那么问题可能并不在“酒”，而在“皮囊”。马耳他总教区的牧灵计划也明确把圣召人数减少、平信徒更充分活出圣洗使命、文化多元化以及数字世界的变化列为当前需要面对的现实。

而这个问题不只是属于主教、神父或者教会委员会。

它也属于我们每一个人。

今天我们可以问自己一个很具体的问题：

**在我的生命里，有没有什么地方，我一直要求耶稣来适应我，而不是允许祂改变我？**

也许是一段我们始终不肯修复的关系。

也许是一个已经知道不诚实，却一直告诉自己“大家都是这样做”的工作习惯。

也许是我们对某个人形成了多年的判断，因此无论对方怎样改变，我们都不愿重新看他。

也许是我们的信仰已经变成固定程序：弥撒、祈祷、节日都还在，但已经很久没有真正问过：“主，你今天希望我改变什么？”

新皮囊并不意味着每天追求新鲜事物。

它意味着一颗**仍然能够被天主改变的心**。

所以今天，我们不需要给自己列十项改革计划。只要选择一个地方就够了：一个旧习惯、一段僵硬的关系、一个根深蒂固的判断，或者一个我们一直不愿交给天主的领域。

然后简单地问：

**主，如果你的福音真的进入这里，我需要改变什么？**

新酒已经赐给了我们。问题不是基督有没有新的生命可以给我们，而是我们的心是否还柔软到能够承载祂。

愿我们不是只把耶稣补在原来的生活上，而是真正让祂更新我们的生命。

### English

In today's Gospel, people put what seems to be a perfectly reasonable question to Jesus: John the Baptist's disciples fast, the disciples of the Pharisees fast, so why are your disciples eating and drinking?

On the surface, the question is about fasting. At a deeper level, however, they are asking: **What should a truly religious person look like?**

They already have a familiar standard. They know when people are supposed to fast. They know what religious devotion is supposed to look like. And they know how to judge whether someone fits the pattern.

Jesus does not answer by arguing about whether people should fast more or fast less. Instead, he moves the question somewhere deeper: **the bridegroom is here.**

That is the heart of the passage.

Christianity begins not with a system of rules but with the presence of a person: Jesus Christ. Fasting, prayer, liturgy, sacrifice and works of charity all matter. But they exist to bring us into communion with Christ. If we forget that, even good religious practices can become an empty shell.

Jesus therefore speaks about a new patch on an old garment and new wine in old wineskins.

It is tempting to reduce his words to a simple slogan: new things are good and old things are bad. But Jesus himself prevents such an easy interpretation. At the end of the passage he observes that the person accustomed to old wine says that the old is good.

That is a remarkably realistic description of us.

We like what is familiar. We like the prayers we already know, the Mass time we are accustomed to, the people we know and the reassuring phrase, "We have always done it this way." The same thing happens in our personal lives. We want God to help us, but preferably without changing us too much. We want Jesus to enter our lives, provided that he fits into the arrangements we have already made.

And so we can end up doing exactly what the image of the patch suggests: **instead of allowing the Gospel to change our life, we cut out a little piece of religion and sew it onto the life we already have.**

We go to Mass on Sunday but conduct our business on Monday exactly as before.

We ask God to forgive us while refusing to release someone else from an old offence.

In church we pray that God's will be done, while at home insisting that everyone must follow our will.

We hear Jesus speak about the poor, the stranger and the vulnerable, yet still classify and dismiss people almost instantly when they disturb our comfort.

A little religion has been added, but the underlying structure has not changed.

The harder form of conversion is not simply learning a new teaching. It is allowing God to reshape the "wineskin" that must carry the Gospel: our habits, our judgments, our relationships, our use of time and money, and sometimes even our understanding of who we are.

There is a particularly relevant example here in Malta.

The Archdiocese of Malta is currently developing its **2026–2029 Pastoral Plan**. Church entities are now in the planning stage, with implementation expected to begin from October 2026. Significantly, the Archdiocese says that the purpose is not simply to create a large number of new initiatives. The aim is a qualitative renewal of ecclesial culture and pastoral practice, including a review of existing ways of working so that communion, participation and mission can be lived more authentically in Malta today.

That sounds remarkably close to the question of new wine and new wineskins.

The Church does not renew herself by throwing away everything that is old. The sacraments, Scripture, the Creed and the apostolic faith are not obsolete wineskins to be discarded. They are part of the treasure entrusted to the Church.

What continually requires renewal is **the way we carry and live that faith**.

If society changes but our pastoral habits can never change; if Catholic communities in Malta increasingly include people from different nations, languages and cultures but we still imagine the parish only through the cultural pattern of the past; if lay Catholics possess many gifts but mission is still treated as something belonging almost entirely to priests and religious, then perhaps the problem is not the wine but the wineskin.

The Maltese Pastoral Plan itself identifies realities such as declining priestly and religious vocations, the need for lay people to live their baptismal calling more fully, growing cultural plurality and the changing digital environment as challenges requiring pastoral renewal.

But today's Gospel is not addressed only to bishops, priests or pastoral councils.

It is addressed to us.

And it leaves us with a very personal question:

**Where in my life am I asking Jesus to adapt himself to me instead of allowing him to change me?**

Perhaps it is a relationship we have refused to repair.

Perhaps it is a dishonest practice at work that we continue to excuse by saying that everyone does it.

Perhaps we formed a judgment about someone years ago, and no matter how much that person changes, we refuse to see them differently.

Perhaps even our faith has become a routine. Mass is still there. Prayer is still there. Feast days are still there. But it has been a long time since we seriously asked, "Lord, what do you want to change in me today?"

A new wineskin does not mean chasing whatever happens to be new.

It means having **a heart that is still capable of being changed by God**.

So perhaps we do not need ten resolutions today. One is enough. Choose one old habit, one hardened relationship, one deeply rooted judgment, or one part of life that you have kept away from God.

And ask a simple question:

**Lord, if your Gospel truly enters this part of my life, what has to change?**

The new wine has already been given. Christ does not lack new life to give us. The question is whether our hearts remain supple enough to receive it.

May we not merely attach Jesus to the life we already have. May we allow him to make our life new.
